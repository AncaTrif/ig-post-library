A Romanian must keep a few sarmale (cabbage rolls) in the freezer, or she is not a Romanian 🥬❄️ Mine are rolled and frozen, waiting for the day I need them. Thanks mum! 🤍

The idea of wrapping food in leaves came to us through the Ottomans. The word sarma comes from the Turkish sarmak, "to wrap". Romanians made it their own with sour cabbage, pickled in autumn so the rolls are ready for winter, for Christmas tables, weddings and baptisms. There are hundreds of ways to make them, and every home changes a little.

Then there is the mămăligă (mum-uh-LEE-guh) underneath, our Romanian polenta, and it is older than the corn in it. Long before maize arrived from the Americas, this porridge was made with millet, a grain that grows where the soil is poor. The Romans knew a millet porridge called pulmentum, and the story goes that they ate so much of it that the Greeks called them porridge eaters. Maize reached Europe in the 16th century and slowly took over the pot. In the Romanian lands it helped feed people through the hungry 17th and 18th centuries, and as a named dish it only shows up in writing in the early 1700s. It was cooked in a cast iron pot, the ceaun, and it was so simple and cheap that even the poorest family could have it. So every time I stir polenta, I am stirring something much older than the yellow grain I pour in.

On a Tuesday I take the uncooked rolls straight from the fridge into the Dutch oven, under a tomato sauce spiced with paprika, cumin, thyme and oregano. They simmer gently for 1½ to 2 hours. If you want to do it in the correct order, the mămăligă goes on the plate first, the sarmale on top, and pickled cucumbers or a spoon of sour cream will round up the dish. 

A bag in the freezer is a whole tradition, waiting for a weeknight. Poftă bună! 🤍
